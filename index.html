About me

I started building aircraft before I knew how to analyse them. Radio-controlled models and FPV quadcopters taught me more in a few seasons than any textbook could, mostly because they kept breaking. Every crash was a diagnosis: a mount that flexed where I had assumed it was rigid, a battery sagging under a load I had never measured, a receiver placed somewhere convenient rather than somewhere sensible. That habit of taking a failure apart until I understand it is the reason I chose mechanical and mechatronic engineering, and it is still how I work.

University turned that instinct into method. On UTS Motorsports Electric I learned what it means to design inside a system I do not control, where a bracket has to satisfy aerodynamics, regulations, manufacturing and a deadline at the same time. Founding AERIS UAV pushed me further in a different direction. Running a 47-member society taught me that the hardest part of a multidisciplinary build is rarely the engineering; it is making sure the mechanical, electrical and software work converge on the same aircraft. I also learned that leading does not mean holding every decision. When somebody on the team knows an area better than I do, the useful thing I can offer is ownership and integration, not instruction.

My internship at Fliight Technologies changed what I consider a finished design. Working on ruggedised ground control hardware, compact radio layouts and off-grid mesh communications meant spending time in the field rather than in CAD, and the field is unforgiving of assumptions. An antenna that performs on the bench can disappoint on a ridge line; an enclosure that seals in the workshop can admit water in the rain. I came away treating information as something to be verified and managed deliberately, checking specifications and tolerances against each other before parts are made rather than discovering the conflict afterwards.

My capstone brought both halves of that together, and it is also where I first had to apply professional judgement to my own ambition. Designing a fully 3D-printed 4+1 VTOL flying wing has meant iterating the fuselage, wings, motor mounts and avionics bay around the strengths and limitations of additive manufacturing. But as the airframe grew heavier, so did the consequences of a failed test. Choosing to seek supervision and a suitable testing environment, rather than flying it myself because I was impatient to see it work, taught me more about being an engineer than any part of the design did.

I finish my degree in December 2026. I want to work where mechanical design, electronics and flight testing meet, in aerospace, defence or advanced technology, and I want to keep working on things that have to survive contact with the real world.

I am writing to apply for the Industrus Engineering Graduate Program. I enjoy working on multidisciplinary projects where mechanical design, electronics and problem-solving come together, and I am particularly interested in Industrus because of the opportunity to rotate through different projects and continue developing both my technical and professional skills.

A commitment to ethical conduct and the highest standards of professional accountability

I have developed a strong understanding of professional accountability through my engineering projects, particularly my capstone project involving the design of a 4+1 VTOL unmanned aircraft. As the aircraft developed, its estimated take-off weight increased to around 2.7 kg due to structural edits, making testing more serious than the smaller UAVs I had previously worked with. Rather than attempting flight testing independently, I recognised that testing needed to take place in a suitable environment with appropriate guidance. I raised this with an industry professional at my internship and looked into alternative testing options where they would support and guide the flight test. This experience reinforced the importance of recognising my own limitations, communicating risks clearly and making decisions based on safety rather than simply trying to progress a project as quickly as possible.

Demonstrated ability to effectively communicate both with other engineers and with stakeholders from different fields

My work on a portable ground control station for the UTS Rover Team has given me experience communicating with both engineers and project stakeholders. Our team needed to convert the client's requirements into a practical system containing three monitors, controls, electronics, power equipment and a laptop connection inside a portable case. My role as system specialist and stakeholder liaison meant I regularly communicated design decisions with my team while also explaining concepts to the client, including mounting methods, manufacturing tolerances and why we wanted to avoid permanently modifying their equipment before the design had been validated. This helped us refine the design while keeping it aligned with what the client actually needed. It also taught me to adjust the amount of technical detail I use depending on who I am speaking with.

The ability to engage with a creative, innovative and proactive environment

I enjoy situations where there is not necessarily one correct solution. My capstone UAV project has involved designing a lightweight, 3D-printed VTOL aircraft that must be aerodynamic, structurally sound, easy to assemble and practical to manufacture on desktop 3D printers. This has required several iterations of the fuselage, wings, motor mounts, avionics bay and structural connections to optimise for endurance. I have actively looked for ways to simplify components, reduce unnecessary material and design parts around the strengths and limitations of additive manufacturing. For example, I have changed component orientation and geometry to improve strength along the print layer direction rather than simply accepting a design that was technically printable with supports. The project has taught me to question whether a design can be improved rather than settling for the first solution that works.

Demonstrated ability to use and manage information

A large part of my UAV work involves gathering and managing technical information from many different sources. When selecting components, I compare motor and propeller specifications, battery requirements, electrical compatibility, physical dimensions, mounting patterns and system constraints before making a decision. I then transfer this information into CAD models, bills of materials and subsystem designs so that changes in one area do not create problems elsewhere. Motor dimensions must match the mounting geometry, electrical components must be compatible with the battery and ESC system, and avionics placement must consider both physical space and interference. Keeping this information organised has helped me identify compatibility problems before manufacturing parts, and has shown me the importance of checking technical information rather than relying on assumptions.

The ability to manage your own performance in a professional environment

Working as a tutor at UTS has helped me become more organised and accountable for my own performance. The role requires me to prepare for classes, explain engineering concepts clearly and assist students with different levels of experience while also managing my own university subjects and engineering projects. I have learned that being prepared is particularly important when other people are relying on me for help. If I am unsure about something, I clarify it with the senior teaching team rather than giving students information I am not confident in. Balancing teaching with my other responsibilities has improved my time management and made me more comfortable seeking feedback and recognising areas where I still need to develop.

A demonstrated ability to work as part of a team and to show leadership when required

I have developed my teamwork and leadership skills through founding and serving as President of AERIS UAV, a university society focused on unmanned aircraft and related engineering projects. Working with students from different engineering backgrounds means that projects often involve mechanical design, electronics, software and avionics at the same time. My role involves helping set project goals, dividing responsibilities and making sure the different areas of a project come together successfully. I have also learned that leadership does not mean making every decision myself. When another team member has more experience in an area, I encourage them to take ownership of it while I focus on supporting the team and overall integration. This has helped me become more confident leading when needed while still valuing the knowledge and ideas of others.
